In February 2024, a small team of four engineers in Rotterdam began building a system that remembers conversations for people who cannot remember them. Their first version stored every message and searched it with a simple inverted index. It worked, but answers were slow: the median query took 1.8 seconds, and anything longer than 200 words was usually cut off. In June they replaced the index with a hybrid of keyword search and dense vectors, which brought the median down to 240 milliseconds, at the cost of 40 percent more memory. The real problem was different, though. Users did not want the most similar text; they wanted the correct text, often from months earlier, described in different words. So in October they added a second layer: a small model that rewrote each old message into a compact factual note before storing it. That layer reduced storage by a factor of roughly three and pushed the correct-answer rate from 61 to 78 percent on their internal 240-question set. The team is now deciding whether to keep the system local, on each user's laptop, or move it to a shared server. Local keeps privacy but caps the index at about two gigabytes; shared allows a much larger memory but means the company can read everything.
